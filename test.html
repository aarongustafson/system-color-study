Add your browser to the picture

CSS system colors are names for particular jobs, such as a page’s background (Canvas) and the text on it (CanvasText). The browser supplies the colors. The system color reference explains those jobs; this study looks at what we actually get across browsers and settings.

I started this after running into poor contrast between system colors on Android. If we’re going to use these colors in our designs, we need a better understanding of where they work well and where we need to take more care. Your snapshot can help fill a gap in the published comparisons, even if we already have a different version of your browser.

Start with your usual settings

The page is already measuring the colors your browser supplies. You don’t need to set up a test first. Use “Display mode” to explore the live samples, check your browser and operating system details, then select “Capture light and dark” to save a snapshot of both.

That button requests both CSS color schemes; it doesn’t switch your operating system’s preferences. To explore a different OS theme or contrast setting, change it yourself and take another snapshot.

Optional: change the contrast backdrop

Some system colors are translucent: the surface behind them shows through and affects their contrast. Leave this at “Resolved Canvas” to start with each mode’s page background. Choose white or black if you want to explore those surfaces instead. This changes how we evaluate transparent colors, not the colors the browser supplies.

Identify this browser/system combination

Browsers don’t always report their full version or the correct OS version. Check their “About” screens and correct these estimates before capturing. “Fill available full versions” may supply more detail where the browser allows it; it doesn’t upload anything.

Testing a Windows Contrast theme or a browser’s custom forced palette? Give it a name so we know which colors you chose. The browser can report whether forced colors are active, but not the palette’s name. A name is required when they’re active; otherwise, leave this blank unless you selected a named theme. Try another palette or contrast preference

Forced colors let someone use a limited palette in place of many of the colors a site’s designer chose. Windows Contrast themes are one example. This can make a page easier to read, and system colors let us work with that choice. Learn more about forced colors and Firefox’s forced-color behavior, including its own color overrides on macOS and Linux.

After your normal snapshot, try a different setting and capture again. On Windows, that could be a built-in or custom Contrast theme. On macOS, try Increase Contrast; in Firefox, you can also test its color overrides. Linux themes and mobile accessibility preferences are worth comparing too. A setting that increases contrast doesn’t necessarily activate forced colors—record what you changed in Notes, and let the browser report its state.

Change one setting at a time so we can understand its effect. Use the actual browser and OS settings for contributions; a developer tool’s emulation is useful for checking a design, but isn’t a measurement of someone’s real palette.

Review your latest snapshot

Look for differences between light and dark, and for anything you didn’t expect. These samples stay as they were when you captured them, even if you change the live preview. In forced-color mode, both schemes may use the same palette; matching results aren’t necessarily a mistake.

Check the colors together

Compare the text with its intended surface, rather than judging each swatch on its own. For ordinary text, look for at least 4.5:1 for WCAG AA. Use the appropriate large-text or non-text threshold for other uses. Transparent colors are evaluated over your selected backdrop. These reference samples preserve the colors we’re examining; the rest of the page still follows your preferences.

Share what you found

More real-world observations will help us make better choices about these colors. Once you’ve reviewed your snapshots, select “Export JSON” to download them. Do this before closing the page: nothing is saved automatically.

Review the downloaded file’s browser details and Notes, then Contribute your snapshot on GitHub. Choose a new branch and open a pull request; GitHub may ask you to sign in or create a fork. Your contribution can be reviewed before it joins the published comparisons.

You can also import snapshots from other devices to compare them below before sharing. This session starts empty and doesn’t change the published results. Importing a file doesn’t upload it.

    Color pairs

    Check whether a text-and-surface pairing stays readable across the settings you’ve tried. Green meets all listed thresholds; amber meets some; red meets none. Read the labels to choose the threshold that applies to your use—amber doesn’t automatically rule a pairing out.

    Compare the “Normal” and “Large” text in each saved pairing. If the larger example looks readable but the smaller one doesn’t, check the threshold for the text you actually need to display.

    The face uses that foreground color too: a smile means all five thresholds pass, a neutral face means some pass, and a frown means none pass. Compare its visibility with the non-text result before choosing the pairing for an icon.

    The guide to reading pair contrast explains the thresholds. A passing ratio is one part of evaluating an interface, not a complete WCAG conformance verdict. Try the pairing with your actual text, controls, and surrounding surfaces.
