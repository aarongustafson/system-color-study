System colors, in practice

System colors let us work with people’s color preferences instead of imposing a palette of our own. To use them well, we need to understand what they give us.

Take a snapshot · Download the dataset (JSON) · Study protocol · Repository

Why this study?

CSS has names for colors with particular jobs: Canvas for a page’s background, CanvasText for its text, ButtonText for text on a button, and so on. The browser supplies their values. Those values can change with the browser, operating system, and someone’s preferences. MDN’s system color keywords reference explains each name.

While working on some web components, I ran into poor contrast between system colors on Android. A user also reported similar issues with forced-color modes on Windows. I’d expected the foreground and background colors to work together. That left me wondering how much their values vary elsewhere, and which pairings we can rely on.

This study brings together observations from real browsers so we can make more informed decisions about using these colors. A keyword’s name tells us its purpose; it doesn’t guarantee the result will be readable in every setting.

The 0 snapshots below are saved observations, not measurements of your current browser. They compare light, dark, forced-color, and high-contrast settings side by side. Add a snapshot from your browser if it’s missing—or if you have a different version or set of preferences to contribute.

Color pairs

A color that looks useful on its own may not work with the color behind it. These pairings put text and surfaces together: CanvasText on Canvas, for example, or ButtonText on ButtonFace. Compare HighlightText on Highlight with SelectedItemText on SelectedItem before choosing a selection treatment, keeping each pair’s intended role in mind.

The “Normal” and “Large” lines let you compare the same pairing at different reading sizes. Both use the saved colors, not your browser’s palette. The larger example may look readable when the smaller one doesn’t; check the threshold for the text you’re designing.

The faces use the same foreground color, giving you an icon to compare too. A smile means all five thresholds pass, a neutral face means some pass, and a frown means none pass. Check the non-text threshold when choosing colors for an icon; the text verdicts are there when the example is hard to see.

For ordinary text, WCAG’s AA contrast requirement is at least 4.5:1; large text needs 3:1. AAA asks for more: 7:1 and 4.5:1, respectively. The non-text contrast requirement uses 3:1 where it applies to controls and meaningful graphics. Choose the threshold that fits what you’re designing.

Green meets every listed threshold; amber meets some; red meets none. The labels tell you which. Amber isn’t necessarily a problem for your use, and green isn’t a complete accessibility review. Translucent colors are evaluated over the recorded backdrop; try your actual surface and check the result in your interface.

GrayText describes disabled text, while ButtonBorder describes a boundary. Neither has a single background that makes sense everywhere. Evaluate them in context, rather than treating every low-contrast swatch as a text failure.

Forced colors

In a forced-color mode, the browser replaces many of a site’s colors with a limited palette chosen by the user or browser. People may rely on that palette to read and use the web. System color keywords give our CSS a way to work with it. MDN explains forced colors and their effect on a page.

Windows Contrast themes are a familiar example, but forced colors aren’t limited to Windows. Firefox’s own color overrides can also activate them on macOS and Linux. A setting such as macOS Increase Contrast isn’t, by itself, the same thing. Mozilla documents Firefox’s forced-color behavior and how it differs from a preference for more contrast.

Evaluating forced colors

Look for the relationships your design depends on: text against its surface, a selected option against its neighbors, and a control against the page. A limited palette may make colors you treated as distinct look the same. Don’t make color the only way to tell them apart.

Light and dark can resolve to the same forced palette. “Light & Dark” means we measured both and found no difference, not that one was skipped.
