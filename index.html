System colors, in practice

System colors let us work with people’s color preferences instead of imposing a palette of our own. To use them well, we need to understand what they give us.

Why this study?

CSS has names for colors with particular jobs: Canvas for a page’s background, CanvasText for its text, ButtonText for text on a button, and so on. The browser supplies their values. Those values can change with the browser, operating system, and someone’s preferences. MDN’s system color keywords reference explains each name.

While working on some web components, I ran into poor contrast between system colors on Android. A user also reported similar issues with forced-color modes on Windows. I’d expected paired foreground and background colors to work together. I was wrong, and that left me wondering how much their values vary elsewhere—and which pairings we can rely on.

This study brings together observations from real browsers so we can make more informed decisions when using system colors. A keyword’s name tells us its purpose; it doesn’t guarantee the result will be readable in every setting.

The scope of this study is limited to the default color schemes supplied by browsers and operating systems, including built-in light, dark, and contrast themes, not custom palettes defined by individual users. People who customize their functional colors own that choice and its consequences; this study makes no judgment about what works for them.

This is also not an argument against system colors. Use them, especially within @media (forced-colors: active) queries, to respect people’s color preferences. The goal is to understand how browsers apply these named colors in different contexts, because some pairings do not work well in every context.

Color pairs

A color that looks useful on its own may not work with the color behind it. These pairings put foreground and background together: CanvasText on Canvas, for example, or ButtonText on ButtonFace.

The “Normal” and “Large” lines let you compare the same pairing at different reading sizes. WCAG’s contrast thresholds depend on text size.

The SVG faces use the same foreground color so you can evaluate an icon too. A smile means all five thresholds pass, a neutral face means some pass, and a frown means none pass.

For ordinary text, WCAG’s AA contrast requirement is at least 4.5:1; large text needs 3:1. AAA asks for more: 7:1 and 4.5:1, respectively. The non-text contrast requirement uses 3:1 where it applies to controls and meaningful graphics.

I’ve also color-coded the cells to make it a little easier to scan for sufficient contrast. Green meets every listed threshold; amber meets some; red meets none. Amber isn’t necessarily problematic; consider it a “with caution” signal.

Note: Semi-transparent colors may look different in a pairing than in an individual swatch because their backgrounds differ. These colors are evaluated over the recorded backdrop; a different backdrop can change the result.

GrayText describes disabled text, while ButtonBorder describes a boundary. Neither has a single background that makes sense everywhere. Evaluate them in context, rather than treating every low-contrast swatch as a text failure.

Forced colors

In a forced-color mode, the browser replaces many of a site’s colors with a limited palette chosen by the user or browser. People may rely on that palette to read and use the web. System color keywords give our CSS a way to work with it. MDN explains forced colors and their effect on a page.

Windows Contrast themes, formerly called High Contrast, are a familiar example, but forced colors aren’t limited to Windows. Firefox’s own color overrides can also activate them on macOS and Linux.

Evaluating forced colors

Consider the relationships your design depends on: text against its surface, a selected option against its siblings, and a control against the page. A limited palette may make colors you treated as distinct look the same.

Which pairs held up across these captures?

A useful pair needs more than a good result in one browser or one scheme. These starting points meet AA contrast for normal text (4.5:1) in every recorded observation in each of four categories: light, dark, forced colors, and more contrast.

These are observations from the setups I’ve collected. Your mileage may vary. Text contrast alone doesn’t establish control boundaries, focus visibility, or distinguishable states.
